Pulmonary function tests (PFTs): These tests determine how much air goes into and out of your lungs,
how much air goes from your lungs to your blood and how well your lungs work during physical activity

it helps diagnose asthma, COPD, pulmonary fibrosis and other conditions that affect the lungs and airways.
Testing can also help providers figure out the best treatment.

PFTs are safe. And they aren’t invasive.

What are the different types?

#### 1. Spirometry
to measure lung volume and airflow, You take a deep breath in and then breathe out forcefully through a mouthpiece for as long as you can.
#### 2.Body plethysmography
You enter a see-through plastic box, where machines measure how much air goes into and out of your lungs when you breathe.
You sit in a clear, plastic box and breathe or pant through a mouthpiece. A machine detects pressure or volume changes in the box as you breathe.
#### 3.Lung diffusion testing (DLCO)
to measure how much gas moves from your lungs to your blood, You breathe in a small, safe amount of carbon monoxide. 
A machine then measures how much carbon monoxide your lungs absorb.
#### 4.cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET):-
to see how much oxygen your body uses when you exercise and how much carbon dioxide it produces,
You walk on a treadmill or cycle on a stationary bike. Machines measure your heartbeat, blood pressure and blood oxygen as you exercise.
#### 5.Methacholine challenge (bronchoprovocation test):
to see if your airways are reactive

### How do your prepare p.t for it?
#### Before testing:
- Stop taking your breathing medicines for a short period.
- Wear loose, comfortable clothing that doesn’t squeeze your chest.
- avoid:
  - Any intense physical activity
  - Using tobacco products
  - Eating a heavy meal
  - Consuming caffeine, including coffee, tea, soda pop and chocolate
- Health teaching about the procedure
  - What is done during testing?
    - Breathing through a mouthpiece connected to a machine
    - Breathing in medications or gases
    - Walking on a treadmill or up and down a hallway
    - Riding a stationary bicycle
    - Before the test, your provider will record some personal information. This allows them to determine a typical value for your demographic. They’ll record your Height, Weight, Age, Sex
    - Then, they’ll take you to a room with PFT equipment. All PFTs except exercise tests involve breathing through a mouthpiece with soft clips covering your nose. Your provider will give you specific instructions according to your test.
  - How long does it take?
    - A PFT may take between 15 and 45 minutes to complete.
    - You may have to do the test a few times to get the most accurate results.

### Restrictive vs. Obstructive Lung Diseases

The main difference is what makes breathing difficult:

1. Obstructive lung disease: Difficulty getting air out because airflow is limited.
Ex: Asthma, COPD (chronic bronchitis and emphysema), Bronchiectasis, Bronchiolitis, Small-airway obstruction
2. Restrictive lung disease: Difficulty getting air in because the lungs cannot expand fully.
Ex: Pulmonary fibrosis, Interstitial lung diseases, Chest-wall deformities, e.g. severe kyphoscoliosis, Neuromuscular diseases, e.g. muscular weakness, Severe obesity can cause an extrapulmonary restrictive pattern
> [!NOTE]
> Restrictive disease does not always mean stiff lung tissue. Restriction can also result from problems outside the lungs, such as chest-wall limitation or respiratory muscle weakness.

#### PFT findings:-

|Measurement|Obstructive|Restrictive|
|-|-|-|
|FEV₁|Decreased, often markedly|Decreased|
|FVC|Normal or decreased|Decreased|
|FEV₁/FVC ratio|Decreased|Normal or increased|
|TLC|Normal or increased|Decreased|
|RV|Often increased	Variable|may be decreased|



- FEV₁: Air exhaled during the first second of a forced expiration.
- FVC: Total air forcibly exhaled after a maximal inspiration.
- TLC: Total volume of air in the lungs after maximal inspiration.
- FEV₁/FVC: The proportion of the forced vital capacity exhaled in the first second.

> [!TIP]
> A low FEV₁/FVC ratio suggests obstruction. A low TLC confirms restriction; a low FVC alone does not prove restrictive disease.
